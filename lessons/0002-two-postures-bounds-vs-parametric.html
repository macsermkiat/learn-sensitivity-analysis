Lesson 02 · Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Inference

Two Postures: Worst-Case Bounds vs Parametric Sensitivity

Where this is heading: there are many named sensitivity methods, but they split into just two big families. Once you can tell which family a method belongs to, you already half-understand what its number means — including the E-value from L01.
How to read this lesson. No formulas this time — it is all about a mental map. Each technical term is unpacked in a blue box the first time it shows up. There is one interactive slider that lets you see the trade-off the whole lesson is about.

1The same problem, two honest reactions

Recall the core move from L01: stop asking "is there a hidden confounder?" and start asking "how much would it take to overturn the result?" Once you accept that the no-hidden-confounder assumption might be false, there are exactly two honest ways to proceed.[2]

An everyday analogy. Someone asks how long your commute will take. You can answer two ways. Way A: "Anywhere from 20 minutes to 2 hours" — guaranteed to contain the truth, but so wide it barely helps. Way B: "About 35 minutes, assuming no accident on the bridge" — precise and useful, but only as good as that assumption. Sensitivity analysis has these same two postures.

Posture A — assume nothing

Worst-case bounds

Make no assumption about who got treated. Ask: what is the full range of effects still consistent with the data?

  • Output is a range, not a single number.
  • Honest and assumption-free.
  • Usually wide — often includes "no effect," so it feels uninformative.
  • Classic version: Manski's nonparametric bounds.[1]
Posture B — assume a little, then dial

Parametric / "how-much" SA

Keep a simple model of how bias works, turn a dial for the confounder's strength, and watch where the estimate moves.

  • Output is a threshold ("the strength that would tip it").
  • Crisp and decision-friendly.
  • Needs judgement: is a confounder that strong plausible?
  • Classic versions: E-value, robustness value, Rosenbaum Γ.
In plain words Worst-case bounds (also called partial identification) means: rather than commit to one number, you report the whole interval of effects the data cannot rule out. Parametric / calibration SA means: you commit to a small model of the bias and report a single tipping-point number. "Parametric" just signals "we assumed a specific functional form for the bias."
The spectrum. Picture a dial running left to right. Far left: no assumptions — wide, bullet-proof, often too vague to act on. Far right: strong assumptions — narrow, precise, only as trustworthy as those assumptions. Every method is a choice of where to stand on this dial. A good paper says where it stood, and why.

2Worst-case bounds, made concrete

Manski's insight: even with zero assumptions about who got treated, the data still pin the average effect to a range.[1] Why? You only ever observe each patient under the one treatment they actually received. For the treatment they did not receive, you fill in the missing outcome with its most extreme possible values — best case for the top of the range, worst case for the bottom. That gives an upper and lower edge.

In plain words ATE = "average treatment effect," the average difference in outcome if everyone were treated vs. if no one were. The bounds are a range for this ATE. They are called sharp when you cannot make them any narrower without adding an assumption — they already squeeze the data for everything it is worth.

For a yes/no or otherwise bounded outcome, these honest bounds are usually wide enough to include "no effect." That is not the method failing — it is the method telling you the truth about how little the data alone can say. To get a narrower range, you have to buy it with an assumption. The slider below shows three common purchases.

Decoding the slider's assumptions Monotone treatment response = "the treatment never hurts anyone" (it can help or do nothing, but not harm). Monotone selection = "sicker patients were the more likely ones to get treated." Instrument = a variable that nudges treatment but has no direct path to the outcome (its "exclusion restriction"), which sharply tightens the range. Each one is plausible in some settings and indefensible in others — that is the whole point.

3Where the E-value (and all the rest) sit

The E-value from L01 is squarely Posture B. It does not give a range; it reports the confounder strength that would erase the result — a single calibrated tipping point.[3] The entire right half of the spectrum has this same shape. The methods differ only in what they dial and on which scale:

In plain words Posture B splits again into two sub-kinds. Analysis-based methods work on the estimate you already computed (any regression or ML model). Design-based methods exploit a special feature of the study design (such as matched pairs, or a control variable). Same posture, different machinery.
Analysis-based (calibrate a model)

E-value · Robustness value

  • E-value dials a risk-ratio strength on both legs (L01).
  • Robustness value dials a "share of variation explained" on both legs (L04).
  • Both work on any adjusted estimate, including ML.
Design-based (exploit the design)

Rosenbaum Γ · Negative controls

  • Γ dials how far assignment departs from random in matched sets (L03).
  • Negative controls detect a confounding footprint (L05).
  • Need a specific design (matching; a valid control variable).
Reading reflex. When you meet any sensitivity analysis, ask two questions in order: (1) Is it a bound (a range, no assumptions) or a calibration (a threshold, with an assumed bias model)? (2) If it is a calibration, is it analysis-based (E-value, robustness value) or design-based (Γ, negative control)? Those two answers locate the method on the map and tell you what its number means.

Quick decoder — every term from this lesson

ATE (average treatment effect)
The average outcome difference between "everyone treated" and "no one treated." The quantity most sensitivity analyses are about.
Worst-case bounds / partial identification
Posture A: report the whole range of effects the data can't rule out, assuming nothing about treatment selection. Honest but often wide.
Sharp bounds
Bounds you cannot narrow further without adding an assumption — already as tight as the raw data allow.
Parametric / calibration SA
Posture B: assume a simple bias model, then report a single tipping-point number (e.g. the E-value).
Analysis-based vs design-based
Two flavours of Posture B: works on the fitted estimate (analysis-based) vs. exploits a study-design feature like matching (design-based).
Monotone treatment response / selection
Sample tightening assumptions: "treatment never harms" / "sicker patients were likelier to be treated."
Instrument
A variable that shifts treatment but has no direct effect on the outcome; if valid, it sharply narrows the bounds.

Hint: two tells — "no assumptions" and a range as the output.
"A confounder would need risk-ratio associations of 3.4 with both exposure and outcome to nullify the effect." Which posture, and which sub-family?
Hint: a single tipping-point on the risk-ratio scale — you met this exact statistic in L01.
