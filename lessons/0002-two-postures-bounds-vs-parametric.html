Lesson 02 · Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Inference

Two Postures: Worst-Case Bounds vs Parametric Sensitivity

Mission tie-in: to "name the families and say when each applies," you first need the top-level split. Every method you'll meet — including the E-value from L01 — sits on one spectrum. Place a method on it and you already half-understand it.

1One assumption, two ways to react to losing it

L01 fixed the core move: stop asking "is there unmeasured confounding?" and start asking "how much would it take to overturn the result?" There are exactly two honest ways to answer once you admit conditional ignorability might fail.[2]

Posture A — drop the assumption

Worst-case bounds

Assume nothing about selection. Ask: what is the full range of effects still consistent with the data?

  • Output is an interval, not a number.
  • Honest and assumption-free.
  • Usually wide — often spans the null, so "uninformative."
  • Canonical: Manski's nonparametric bounds.[1]
Posture B — calibrate the violation

Parametric / "how-much" SA

Keep a model of the bias, dial a confounder's strength, and read off where the estimate moves.

  • Output is a threshold ("strength needed to tip").
  • Crisp and actionable.
  • Requires judgement: is that strength plausible?
  • Canonical: E-value, robustness value, Rosenbaum Γ.
The spectrum. Picture a dial. Far left: no assumptions — wide, bullet-proof, often useless. Far right: strong parametric assumptions — narrow, precise, only as trustworthy as the assumptions. Every method is a choice of where to stand. A good paper states where, and why.

2Worst-case bounds, concretely

Manski's insight: even with zero assumptions about who got treated, the data plus the logical range of the outcome pin the average effect to an interval.[1] You observe outcomes only under the treatment each unit actually received; the counterfactual arm is filled in with its most extreme admissible values to get the upper and lower edge.

These bounds are sharp — you cannot narrow them without adding an assumption — but for a binary or bounded outcome they are typically wide enough to straddle zero. That is not a failure; it is the honest statement of how little the data alone can say. You then buy a narrower interval with assumptions: monotone treatment response, monotone selection, or an instrument.[2]

3Where the E-value (and the rest) sit

The E-value is firmly Posture B. It does not bound the effect; it reports the confounder strength that would erase it — a calibrated tipping point.[3] The whole right half of the spectrum shares that shape; they differ in what they dial and on which scale:

Analysis-based (calibrate a model)

E-value · Robustness value

  • E-value dials a risk-ratio association on both legs (L01).
  • Robustness value dials partial-R² on both legs (L04).
  • Both work on any adjusted estimate, including ML.
Design-based (exploit the design)

Rosenbaum Γ · Negative controls

  • Γ dials departure from random assignment in matched sets (L03).
  • Negative controls detect a confounding footprint (L05).
  • Need a specific design (matching; a valid control variable).
Reading reflex. When you meet any sensitivity analysis, ask two questions in order: (1) Is it a bound (interval, no assumptions) or a calibration (threshold, assumed bias model)? (2) If calibration, is it analysis-based (E-value, RV) or design-based (Γ, negative control)? Those two answers locate the method and tell you what its number means.

4Check yourself

Classify each scenario by posture. Feedback is immediate.

A paper reports: "Under no assumptions about treatment selection, the ATE lies in [−0.04, 0.21]." Which posture?
"A confounder would need risk-ratio associations of 3.4 with both exposure and outcome to nullify the effect." Posture, and which sub-family?
A reviewer dismisses a Manski-bounds analysis: "The interval is so wide it includes zero — useless." Best response?
Which statement about the spectrum is correct?
Your win: you can place any sensitivity analysis on the spectrum — bound vs calibration, and within calibration, analysis- vs design-based — which tells you what its number means before you read a single formula.
