Lesson 01 · Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Inference

What a Sensitivity Analysis Actually Asks

Where this is heading: by the end you can pick up a clinical study, find the one number it reports for "robustness to unmeasured confounding" (the E-value), and judge whether the claim is earned or oversold. That one skill covers most sensitivity analyses you will ever meet.
How to read this lesson. It assumes only that you have seen observational studies report an effect (a risk ratio, an odds ratio). Every technical term is unpacked in plain words the first time it appears, in a blue box like the ones below. Go slowly; there is one idea here, repeated from three angles.

1Start with a worry you already have

Picture a normal observational study — the kind you read all the time:

A cohort study follows patients on drug A and patients not on drug A. It finds the drug-A group has twice the rate of heart attacks: a risk ratio of 2.0. The authors conclude drug A raises heart-attack risk.

Before believing "the drug causes heart attacks," you ask the obvious clinician's question: were the two groups really the same to begin with? Suppose the sicker patients were the ones who got drug A. Then their higher heart-attack rate might come from being sicker, not from the drug. Baseline sickness is riding along with treatment and pushing the outcome up on its own.

In plain words A confounder is a third thing that (a) differs between your treated and untreated groups and (b) affects the outcome on its own. It creates a fake — or inflated — association between treatment and outcome. "Baseline sickness" is the confounder in our story.

The usual fix: adjust for the confounders you measured — age, prior disease, lab values — with regression, matching, or weighting. After adjustment, the comparison is meant to be apples-to-apples. But here is the catch that never goes away:

The thing you can never rule out. What about a confounder you did not measure — or never even thought of? Call it U. You have no data on it, so you cannot adjust for it, and you cannot prove it does not exist. Every observational causal claim rests on the hope that no important U is lurking.
In plain words "No important U is lurking" has a formal name: conditional ignorability (also called no unmeasured confounding). "Conditional" = after adjusting for what you measured; "ignorability" = you can then treat who-got-treated as essentially random. It is an assumption, never something your data can check.

2The reframe that makes the problem solvable

You cannot prove U does not exist. So stop trying to. Sensitivity analysis swaps the impossible question for an answerable one:

The reframe. Not "is there unmeasured confounding?" (you can never know) but "how strong would an unmeasured confounder have to be to overturn my conclusion?" If the honest answer is "implausibly strong," your result stands. If "barely any," it is fragile. That single number is the whole game.

Think of it as a stress test. You are not asking whether the bridge will be hit by a truck; you are asking how heavy a truck it would take to make the bridge fall. A bridge that only fails under an impossibly heavy truck is one you trust.

In plain words Sensitivity analysis = a family of methods that all compute some version of "how strong would the hidden confounder have to be to explain my result away." Every tool in this course — E-values, Rosenbaum Γ, contour plots, robustness values for machine-learning estimators — is a different way of producing and calibrating that one "how strong" number. Learn the reframe once and every method is a variation on it.

3The E-value: the "how strong" number you will see most

In clinical real-world-data papers, the dominant version of that number is the E-value.[1] Read this definition slowly, twice:

The E-value is the smallest strength of association that an unmeasured confounder U would need to have with both the treatment and the outcome — over and above the variables you already adjusted for — to fully explain away the result (drag it all the way back to "no effect").
In plain words "Strength of association" here is just a risk ratio. An E-value of 3.4 means: a hidden confounder would have to make the outcome about 3.4× more likely and make getting the treatment about 3.4× more likely, at the same time, before it could account for the whole finding. Bigger E-value = more confounding required = more robust. An E-value of 1 means "zero confounding required" — the result was already null.

It is computed straight from the point estimate. For a risk ratio of 1 or more:

E = RR + √( RR · (RR − 1) )
(if your RR is below 1 — a protective effect — first flip it: use RR* = 1 / RR, then apply the same formula)

Let us actually plug in the heart-attack study's RR = 2.0, one step at a time:

E = 2.0 + √( 2.0 × (2.0 − 1) ) ← start with RR = 2.0
E = 2.0 + √( 2.0 × 1.0 ) ← 2.0 − 1 = 1.0
E = 2.0 + √( 2.0 ) ← 2.0 × 1.0 = 2.0
E = 2.0 + 1.41 ← √2.0 ≈ 1.41
E = 3.41 ← the E-value

So to explain away the drug-A finding, a hidden confounder would need roughly 3.4-fold links with both taking drug A and having a heart attack. Compare that to a weaker finding, RR = 1.3, which gives only E = 1.92 — a much smaller confounder would erase it, so it is far easier to dismiss. Play with it:

4Reading one in the wild — four reflexes

Being fluent here is mostly about not getting fooled. When you see an E-value in a paper, run these four checks:

1. Benchmark it — a number alone means nothing. An E-value of 3.4 is only impressive if a confounder that strong is implausible. Compare it to the strength of the confounders the authors did measure and adjust for. If a hidden confounder no stronger than one they already controlled would clear the bar, the result is fragile — no matter how big the raw E-value looks.[3]

2. Read the CI-limit E-value, not just the point one. Good papers report a pair: one E-value for the estimate, and one for the end of the confidence interval closest to "no effect."

In plain words The CI-limit E-value asks the gentler question "how much confounding to make the result merely non-significant?" (rather than "to erase it entirely"). A study can have an impressive point E-value of 3.4 but a CI-limit E-value of only 1.4 — meaning quite weak confounding is enough to push it past the significance line. The CI-limit number is usually the more honest one.

3. It is not a probability. An E-value of 4 does not mean "a 1-in-4 chance the result is spurious." It is a strength threshold, and it says nothing at all about whether a confounder that strong actually exists in the world.

4. Both legs, at once. The confounder has to hit the threshold with the treatment and with the outcome simultaneously. Something strongly linked to the outcome but only weakly linked to treatment (or vice versa) produces far less bias than the E-value's single number suggests.

Quick decoder — every term from this lesson

Confounder
A third variable that differs between treated/untreated groups and also affects the outcome — fakes or inflates the association.
U (unmeasured confounder)
A confounder you have no data on, so you could not adjust for it. The thing sensitivity analysis worries about.
Conditional ignorability / no unmeasured confounding
The untestable assumption that, after adjusting for what you measured, treatment is as-good-as random.
Sensitivity analysis
Methods that ask how strong a U would have to be to overturn the result — instead of whether U exists.
Risk ratio (RR)
How many times more (or less) likely the outcome is in the treated vs. untreated group. RR = 2 means twice as likely; RR = 1 means no difference.
E-value
The smallest confounder strength (on the RR scale, with both treatment and outcome) needed to fully explain the result away. Bigger = more robust.
CI-limit E-value
The same idea applied to the confidence-interval edge nearest the null: how little confounding to make the result non-significant.
Benchmarking
Judging an E-value by comparing it to the strength of the confounders that were actually measured and adjusted for.
