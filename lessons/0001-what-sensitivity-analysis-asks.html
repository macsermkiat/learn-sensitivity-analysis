Lesson 01 · Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Inference

What a Sensitivity Analysis Actually Asks

Mission tie-in: by the end you can pick up any clinical RWD paper, read its E-value, and judge whether "robust to unmeasured confounding" is earned or overstated. That single skill covers most sensitivity analyses you'll meet.

1The one question behind all of it

Every observational causal estimate — a regression coefficient, a matched odds ratio, a doubly-robust ATE — rests on one assumption you can never check: conditional ignorability, i.e. no unmeasured confounding. Given the covariates you measured, treatment is as-good-as-random.[1]

You cannot test that assumption with your data. So sensitivity analysis flips the question from the unanswerable to the answerable:

The reframe. Not "is there unmeasured confounding?" (untestable) but "how strong would unmeasured confounding have to be to overturn my conclusion?" If the answer is "implausibly strong," the result stands. If "barely any," it is fragile. That number is the whole game.

This is the mental model. Every tool in this field — E-values, Rosenbaum Γ-bounds, omitted-variable-bias contour plots, robustness values for causal ML — is a different way of producing and calibrating that "how strong" number. Learn the reframe once and every method becomes a variation on a theme.

2The E-value: the number you'll see most

In clinical RWD, the dominant instantiation is the E-value [1]. Definition, worth memorising:

The E-value is the minimum strength of association, on the risk-ratio scale, that an unmeasured confounder would need with both the treatment and the outcome — beyond the measured covariates — to fully explain away the observed association.

One number, computed from the point estimate:

for RR ≥ 1  →  E = RR + √( RR · (RR − 1) )
(if RR < 1, use RR* = 1/RR first)

So an observed RR = 2.0 gives E = 2 + √(2·1) = 3.41: a confounder would need roughly 3.4-fold associations with both the exposure and the outcome to nullify it. An RR = 1.3 gives only E = 1.92 — much easier to explain away. Larger E-value, more robust; an E-value of 1 means the result is already null.

3Reading one in the wild — the critique checklist

Fluency is mostly about not being fooled. Four reflexes when you see an E-value:

1. Benchmark it. An E-value is meaningless in isolation. Compare it to the strength of the covariates you did measure. If a confounder weaker than ones already adjusted-for would suffice, the result is fragile — regardless of the raw number.[3]

2. Read the CI-limit E-value too. Always reported as a pair: one for the estimate, one for the confidence bound nearest the null. A big point E-value with a small CI-limit E-value means the result tips out of significance under weak confounding.[1]

3. It is not a probability. An E-value of 4 is not "a 1-in-4 chance of being spurious." It is a strength threshold, and says nothing about whether a confounder that strong actually exists.

4. Both legs, jointly. The confounder must hit the threshold with treatment and outcome simultaneously. Strong on one leg, weak on the other produces far less bias than the E-value implies.

4Check yourself

Pick an answer — feedback is immediate.

A study reports adjusted RR = 1.3, E-value 1.9. The strongest measured confounder had ~2.5-fold associations with the outcome. Robust to unmeasured confounding?

Your win: you now hold the reframe (SA = the strength of confounding needed to overturn a result) and can read, compute, and critique an E-value — the most common sensitivity statistic in clinical RWD.
