Lesson 03 · Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Inference

Rosenbaum Γ-Bounds for Matched Designs

Mission tie-in: matched-cohort and case-control RWD studies report robustness as a Γ, not an E-value. To critique them you must read Γ correctly — and know the one trap that fools most readers.

1The setup: randomization-within-a-match, broken a little

Rosenbaum's framework lives in matched designs — pairs or sets of units identical on the measured covariates, differing in treatment.[1] If matching captured every confounder, then within a matched set treatment would be as-good-as-randomized: each unit equally likely to be the treated one. The test (sign-rank, McNemar, etc.) assumes exactly that.

Hidden bias means an unmeasured U tilts those odds. Γ (gamma) is the dial on that tilt:

1/Γ  ≤  odds(treated | unit i) / odds(treated | unit j)  ≤  Γ
for any two units i, j in the same matched set (identical on measured covariates)
Read Γ like this. Γ = 1: matching worked, assignment is random within sets — no hidden bias. Γ = 2: an unmeasured factor could make one matched unit up to twice as likely as its partner to be treated. The larger Γ can grow before your result loses significance, the more robust the finding.[2]

2What the analysis actually computes

For each candidate Γ, Rosenbaum derives the worst-case distribution of the test statistic — the most adverse way that bias of magnitude Γ could be hiding — and from it a worst-case (upper-bound) p-value.[1] As Γ rises from 1, that p-value rises. The reported number is the Γ at which the worst-case p-value crosses your α — the point where the conclusion is no longer safe from hidden bias.

The classic demonstration is smoking and lung cancer: in matched data the association stays significant out to very large Γ (around 5–6).[2] An unmeasured confounder would have to make smokers many times more likely to smoke and push the outcome hard — wildly implausible — which is precisely why an observational result is treated as causal.

3The trap: Γ is only the treatment leg

This is where most readers slip. Γ bounds only how much U distorts the odds of treatment. It says nothing, by itself, about how strongly U drives the outcome.[1] To actually bias the estimate, a confounder must move both — exactly like the E-value's two-leg requirement (L01).

Amplification. A reported Γ corresponds to a whole curve of (treatment-leg, outcome-leg) strength pairs that would produce it — Rosenbaum & Silber's amplification into (Λ, Δ). So "insensitive up to Γ = 1.5" means a confounder of moderate strength on both legs would suffice. A bare Γ is harder to interpret than an E-value until you amplify it. Don't read Γ as if it were the full confounder strength.

Practical contrast for your toolkit: Γ is design-based (needs a matched study, lives on the treatment-odds scale) while the E-value is analysis-based (works on any adjusted estimate, lives on the risk-ratio scale). Same spirit as L02's spectrum — both are Posture B calibrations, just different dials.
