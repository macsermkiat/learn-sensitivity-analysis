Lesson 05 · Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Inference

Negative Controls: the Design-Based Detector

Where this is heading: the last family on your map. Negative controls answer a different question from every calibration method — "is there a detectable footprint of confounding at all?" Clinical RWD papers lean on them heavily, and knowing their limits is the fluency test.
How to read this lesson. No formulas — it is about a clever study-design trick and its two failure modes. Each term gets a plain-words box. The interactive widget at the end lets you practise spotting valid vs. broken controls.

1The idea, borrowed from the lab bench

In a wet lab, you run a control you know should come back blank — a test tube with no target in it. If it lights up anyway, you don't celebrate; you conclude your assay is contaminated. The blank is a tripwire for "something is leaking into my measurements."

Lipsitch, Tchetgen Tchetgen & Cohen imported exactly that move into epidemiology.[1] Pick something that cannot be causally affected in your study, but that gets dragged around by the same confounding as your real question — then check whether an effect shows up where none should exist.

In plain words A negative control is a deliberately "should-be-null" relationship you run alongside your real analysis. If it comes back non-null, the most likely explanation is the bias your design failed to remove — residual confounding, selection, or measurement error. It is a contamination check for your causal pipeline.
The logic in one line. A relationship that must be zero, but isn't, is your study confessing that bias is leaking in.

2Two flavours: swap the outcome, or swap the exposure

Swap the outcome

Negative-control outcome (NCO)

An outcome the treatment cannot plausibly cause, but that is driven by the same confounders.

Clinical example: checking whether a drug "causes" some outcome it has no mechanism for, in the same cohort. A non-null hit ⇒ confounding is present.

Swap the exposure

Negative-control exposure (NCE)

An exposure that cannot cause the real outcome, but shares the same confounding structure.

Example: a "treatment" given after the outcome window, or an unrelated prescription that carries the same prescribing biases.

In plain words Same trick, two directions. An NCO keeps your real treatment but swaps in a fake outcome. An NCE keeps your real outcome but swaps in a fake exposure. Either way, the swapped-in thing is chosen so the true effect is known to be zero.

A popular special case is the pre-exposure NCO: an outcome measured before the treatment could have acted. Any "effect" there is impossible by time-order alone, so it cleanly isolates baseline imbalance between the groups.[1]

3The two assumptions that make or break it

A negative control is only as trustworthy as two conditions — and almost every valid critique of one lives in these two boxes:[1]

Assumption 1

The null really holds

The control must have no true causal effect. If the treatment actually does nudge your "negative" outcome a little, a non-null hit is a real effect, not bias — a false alarm.

Assumption 2

U-comparability

The control must share the same confounders as the real analysis. If it's confounded by different things, a clean control falsely reassures you.

In plain words U-comparability just means "same hidden confounders (U) as the real question." The two failure modes are mirror images: Assumption 1 fails → the control wasn't really null, so a hit means nothing. Assumption 2 fails → the control is pushed by different confounders, so a clean result proves nothing about your real analysis.

4Detector, not quantifier — where it sits on your map

Here is the fluency point, and the one to remember. A negative control can reveal that confounding is present (and often hint at its direction), but on its own it does not tell you how big the bias on your real estimate is.[1]

The key distinction Detect ≠ quantify. A smoke alarm detects fire; it does not tell you how much of the house is burning. Negative controls are the smoke alarm. The E-value, robustness value, and Γ are the methods that estimate the size of the problem.
The five families, one line each:
• Worst-case bounds (L02): the honest range with no assumptions.
• E-value (L01) & robustness value (L04): how strong would confounding need to be? (analysis-based calibration)
• Rosenbaum Γ (L03): the same question for matched designs (design-based calibration).
• Negative controls (L05): is there a detectable footprint of confounding at all? (design-based detector)
Calibration methods quantify; negative controls detect. A strong analysis often reports both.

Depth pointer for later: methods called control-outcome calibration and proximal causal inference push negative controls past mere detection into actually correcting the bias — worth recognising the terms when you meet them.[2]

Quick decoder — every term from this lesson

Negative control
A deliberately should-be-null relationship run alongside the real analysis; a non-null result signals leftover bias.
NCO (negative-control outcome)
Keep the real treatment, swap in an outcome it cannot cause but that shares the confounding.
NCE (negative-control exposure)
Keep the real outcome, swap in an exposure that cannot cause it but shares the confounding.
Pre-exposure NCO
An outcome measured before treatment could act; any "effect" is impossible by time-order, so it isolates baseline imbalance.
Null holds (Assumption 1)
The control truly has no causal effect. If false, a hit is a real effect, not bias.
U-comparability (Assumption 2)
The control shares the same hidden confounders as the real analysis. If false, a clean result is meaningless.
Detector vs. quantifier
Negative controls detect a footprint of bias; calibration methods (E-value, RV, Γ) estimate its size.
