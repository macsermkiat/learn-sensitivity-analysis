Lesson 05 · Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Inference

Negative Controls: the Design-Based Detector

Mission tie-in: the last family on your map. Negative controls answer a different question from every calibration method — "is there a detectable footprint of confounding?" — and clinical RWD papers lean on them heavily. Knowing their limits is the fluency test.

1The idea, borrowed from the lab bench

In a wet lab you run a control you know should show nothing. If it lights up, your assay is contaminated. Lipsitch, Tchetgen Tchetgen & Cohen imported this into epidemiology: pick something that cannot be causally affected here but is dragged by the same confounding — then look for an effect that shouldn't exist.[1]

The logic. If a relationship that must be null comes back non-null, the most likely culprit is the bias your design failed to remove — residual confounding, selection, or measurement error. The negative control is a tripwire for "your pipeline is biased."

2Two flavours

Swap the outcome

Negative-control outcome (NCO)

An outcome the exposure cannot plausibly cause, but driven by the same confounders.

Clinical example: testing whether a drug "causes" an outcome it has no mechanism for, in the same cohort. A non-null hit ⇒ confounding.

Swap the exposure

Negative-control exposure (NCE)

An exposure that cannot cause the real outcome, but shares the confounding structure.

Example: a "treatment" given after the outcome window, or an unrelated prescription with the same prescribing biases.

A widely used special case is the pre-exposure NCO: an outcome measured before treatment could act. Any "effect" there is impossible by time-order, so it isolates baseline imbalance.[1]

3The two assumptions that make or break it

A negative control is only as good as two conditions, and most critiques live here:[1]

Assumption 1

The null really holds

The control must have no true causal effect. If the drug actually does nudge your "negative" outcome a little, a non-null hit is real, not bias — false alarm.

Assumption 2

U-comparability

The control must share the same confounders as the real analysis. If it's confounded by different things, a clean control falsely reassures you.

4Detector, not quantifier — where it sits on your map

This is the fluency point. A negative control can reveal that confounding is present (and often its direction), but on its own it does not tell you how big the bias on your real estimate is.[1] Contrast the whole map:

The families, one line each:
• Worst-case bounds (L02): the honest range with no assumptions.
• E-value (L01) & robustness value (L04): how strong would confounding need to be? (analysis-based calibration)
• Rosenbaum Γ (L03): the same question for matched designs (design-based calibration).
• Negative controls (L05): is there a detectable footprint of confounding at all? (design-based detector)
Calibration methods quantify; negative controls detect. A strong analysis often reports both.

Depth pointer: methods like control-outcome calibration and proximal causal inference push negative controls past detection into actually correcting the bias — worth knowing the terms exist when you see them.[2]
