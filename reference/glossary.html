Glossary — Sensitivity Analysis for Causal Inference

Canonical definitions. Reused verbatim across all lessons. Terms grouped: core idea → the tools → design-based checks.
Core idea
Conditional ignorability (a.k.a. no unmeasured confounding, NUC, exchangeability)
The central, untestable assumption of observational causal inference: given the measured covariates, treatment is as-good-as-random with respect to potential outcomes. Every adjusted estimate (regression, matching, IPW, DML) leans on it.
Unmeasured confounder (U)
A variable associated with both the treatment and the outcome that was not measured/adjusted for. Its existence is what breaks conditional ignorability and biases the estimate. Sensitivity analysis is the study of how much such a U would matter.
ATE (average treatment effect)
The average difference in outcome if everyone in the population were treated versus if no one were. The usual causal target that bounds, calibrations, and detectors are all protecting.
Sensitivity analysis
A set of methods that ask: how strong would a violation of the no-unmeasured-confounding assumption have to be to overturn my conclusion? It does not test whether confounding exists; it quantifies the bias required to explain the result away.
Benchmarking
Calibrating "how strong is plausible?" by comparing a hypothesised U against the strength of the measured covariates. "Would U need to be 3× stronger than the strongest variable we did measure?" turns an abstract number into a judgement.
The parametric tools
E-value
The minimum strength of association — on the risk-ratio scale — that an unmeasured confounder would need with both treatment and outcome (above and beyond measured covariates) to fully explain away the observed association. Larger = more robust. An E-value of 1 means none needed (result already null).
Formula: for risk ratio RR ≥ 1, E = RR + √(RR·(RR−1)). For RR < 1, first take RR* = 1/RR, then apply.
CI-limit E-value
The E-value computed for the confidence-interval bound nearest the null, not the point estimate. Answers "how much confounding to make the result non-significant?" Always reported alongside the point E-value; a study can have a large point E-value but a fragile CI-limit one.
Risk-ratio scale (conversions)
E-values are defined on the RR scale. Other estimates are first converted: odds ratio (rare outcome) RR ≈ OR; hazard ratio RR ≈ HR (rare) or via a correction; standardized mean difference RR ≈ exp(0.91·d). Getting the conversion right is essential when reading a paper.
Omitted variable bias (OVB)
The classical regression idea — bias from leaving out a relevant variable — generalised into a sensitivity framework. The bias is bounded by how much of the variation in treatment and in outcome the omitted variable could explain.
Partial R² (treatment-side & outcome-side)
Scale-free sensitivity parameters: the share of residual variation in the treatment, and in the outcome, that the unobserved confounder would explain. Two numbers fully parameterise the worst-case bias (Cinelli & Hazlett). Enables transparent benchmarking against measured covariates.
Robustness value (RV)
A single summary: the minimum partial R² (assumed equal on both the treatment and outcome sides) that confounding would need to reduce the estimate to the null (RVq=1) or to the edge of significance. The OVB-framework analogue of the E-value; extends to ML/DML estimators via dml.sensemakr.
Contour / Austen plot
A 2-D map with treatment-side strength on one axis and outcome-side strength on the other; curves show where the estimate (or its CI) hits the null. Measured covariates are plotted as reference points so you can read robustness at a glance. "Austen plot" is the causal-ML styling (Veitch & Zaveri).
Worst-case bounds (partial identification, Manski)
Instead of one number, report the range of effects consistent with the data under bounded assumptions. Makes no point claim; brackets what is and isn't knowable. The non-parametric counterpart to the parametric tools above.
Design-based checks
Rosenbaum Γ-bound (gamma)
For matched observational designs: Γ measures how much two matched units could differ in their odds of treatment due to an unmeasured U. The analysis reports the Γ at which the result stops being significant. Larger Γ before significance is lost = more robust. Γ bounds only the treatment-assignment leg — not the confounder's effect on the outcome.
Amplification (of Γ; Rosenbaum & Silber)
The translation of a single Γ into the curve of (treatment-leg, outcome-leg) strength pairs that would produce it. Because a bare Γ describes only the treatment leg, amplification is what makes it comparable to E-value / partial-R² two-leg thinking. A given Γ corresponds to many (Λ, Δ) combinations.
Negative control (outcome or exposure)
A variable that should have no true causal relationship in the analysis but shares the same confounding structure. A non-null association on the negative control signals residual confounding. A design-based detector rather than a quantifier.
Two flavours: a negative-control outcome (NCO) keeps the real treatment and swaps in an outcome it cannot cause; a negative-control exposure (NCE) keeps the real outcome and swaps in an exposure that cannot cause it. A pre-exposure NCO (outcome measured before treatment could act) makes the null hold by time-order alone.
U-comparability
The assumption that a negative control shares the same unmeasured confounders (U) as the real analysis. Without it, a clean negative control falsely reassures — it was being pushed by different confounders than the question you care about.
Tipping-point analysis
Vary an assumption (often about missing data or an unmeasured factor) until the conclusion "tips." Report the value at which it tips and judge whether that value is plausible. A general technique; the E-value is essentially a tipping-point for confounding strength.
DML (double / debiased machine learning)
An estimation framework using flexible ML for nuisance functions while preserving valid inference for the causal target. Relevant here because the OVB/robustness-value machinery has been extended to it — so sensitivity analysis is not limited to linear regression.
