The Critique Checklist

Everything from L01–L05 condensed into the questions you ask when a paper claims its result is "robust to unmeasured confounding." Print it, or keep it open beside the paper.

AlwaysWhatever the paper reports

  • Is there a sensitivity analysis at all? An observational causal claim with no sensitivity analysis is a claim resting silently on "no unmeasured confounding." (L01)
  • Which family is it? A bound (range, no assumptions), a calibration (tipping-point number: E-value, RV, Γ), or a detector (negative control)? Naming the family tells you what the number can and cannot mean. (L02)
  • Is the number benchmarked? A raw E-value / RV / Γ means nothing alone. Did the authors compare it to the strength of confounders they did measure? If a hidden confounder no stronger than a measured one would tip the result, it is fragile. (L01, L04)
  • Does the conclusion language match the number? "Highly robust" should be earned by the weakest link (usually the CI-limit value), not the headline one.

E-valueIf the paper reports an E-value (L01)

  • CI-limit E-value reported too? A big point E-value with a small CI-limit E-value means weak confounding already breaks significance — read the pair, trust the smaller.
  • Scale conversion done right? E-values live on the risk-ratio scale. OR/HR with a rare outcome ≈ fine; a common-outcome OR must be converted (√OR) first, or the E-value is inflated.
  • Not read as a probability? E = 4 is a strength threshold, never "a 1-in-4 chance the result is spurious."
  • Both legs, jointly? The confounder must hit the threshold with treatment and outcome at once, beyond measured covariates.

ΓIf the paper reports a Rosenbaum Γ (L03)

  • Is the design actually matched? Γ is a matched-design statistic; quoting one for an unmatched estimate is a category error.
  • How far above 1? Γ = 1.1–1.3 is fragile; smoking–lung-cancer territory is 5–6. Judge the distance, not the existence, of the number.
  • The treatment-leg trap. Γ bounds only the odds of getting treated. Any claim about the confounder's required effect on the outcome needs amplification — a bare Γ is half the story.

RVIf the paper reports a robustness value / contour plot (L04)

  • Both RVs? The null RV and the significance-boundary RV (the analogue of the CI-limit E-value). The smaller one is the honest one.
  • Are measured covariates plotted as benchmarks? A contour plot without covariate dots cannot be judged. If any measured covariate's dot sits past the null curve, an equally ordinary hidden confounder would kill the result.
  • ML is not exempt. A DML / causal-ML estimate needs this exact same reading (dml.sensemakr); flexible models do not remove confounding.

BoundsIf the paper reports worst-case bounds (L02)

  • Which assumptions were purchased? Each narrowing step (monotone response, monotone selection, instrument) must be named and defended, not just applied.
  • Wide is not wrong. Bounds that include "no effect" are the honest statement of what the data alone support — dismissing them as "useless" misses the point.

NCIf the paper reports a negative control (L05)

  • Does the null really hold? If the treatment could plausibly affect the "negative" outcome even a little, a hit is a real effect, not detected bias.
  • Is U-comparability argued? The control must share the same confounders as the real analysis — otherwise a clean control falsely reassures.
  • Detect ≠ quantify. A clean negative control is "no footprint found," never "proof of no confounding"; a dirty one says bias exists, not how big it is.

Instant red flags

  • "Robust" claimed from the point E-value alone, CI-limit ignored.
  • A common-outcome odds ratio plugged into the E-value formula unconverted.
  • Γ presented as if it were the confounder's full strength (both legs).
  • A null negative control presented as proof of no confounding.
  • An E-value or RV reported with no benchmarking against measured covariates anywhere.
  • Any sensitivity number read as a probability.
